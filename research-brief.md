# Research Brief — Project 2

## Research question

What post-class review methods help me understand concepts I missed and the connections between them?

## Why this question matters to my process

When a lecture moves quickly, I record keywords and review the slides afterward. Looking at the slides again does not always help me explain why a concept matters, how it relates to another concept, or how an example demonstrates it. I need a review process that lets me check what I actually understand.

## Source 1: Review of study techniques

John Dunlosky, Katherine A. Rawson, Elizabeth J. Marsh, Mitchell J. Nathan, and Daniel T. Willingham. “Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology.” *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, vol. 14, no. 1, 2013. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

The authors reviewed evidence for ten learning techniques. They rated practice testing and studying across separate sessions as broadly useful. They rated self-explanation as moderately useful and rereading and summarization as lower in overall utility. Their review does not mean every summary is useless; it means the evidence for summarization is less consistently strong across learners and tasks than the evidence for practice testing. The authors also note that research on the durability and classroom use of self-explanation is more limited.

**Implication for my workflow:** Instead of only rereading slides, I can try to explain a concept and its connection to an example in my own words, then answer a question without looking at the slides. I can check my answer against the course material and revisit the question later.

## Source 2: Retrieval practice and concept mapping

Jeffrey D. Karpicke and Janell R. Blunt. “Retrieval Practice Produces More Learning Than Elaborative Studying With Concept Mapping.” *Science*, vol. 331, no. 6018, 2011, pp. 772–775. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327

In the study's learning tasks, practicing retrieval produced better later learning outcomes than elaborative study with a concept map. This comparison does not show that visual maps are never helpful. It suggests that making a map while looking at the material may not reveal what I can explain from memory.

**Implication for my workflow:** I can still use a concept–example–connection layout to organize the review guide. Then I should close the guide and reconstruct the connections or answer a short question from memory. Afterward, I should compare my attempt with the original slides and notes.

## Proposed review process to test

1. Gather the lecture slides and the keywords I wrote during class. Mark what I missed or cannot explain.
2. Organize the relevant concepts, examples, and possible connections. Mark uncertain connections as questions rather than facts.
3. Explain the important connections in my own words, without looking at a prepared answer first.
4. Answer a few review questions from memory; check my answers against the lecture materials and correct errors.
5. Return to the difficult questions in a later study session.

I will decide which concepts matter for my course, whether a proposed connection matches what the instructor taught, and whether my own explanation makes sense. The studies inform this process, but they do not test this exact workflow in my English-language lectures. I will judge its usefulness by whether I can explain the concepts and their connections afterward, not merely recognize them on a slide.
